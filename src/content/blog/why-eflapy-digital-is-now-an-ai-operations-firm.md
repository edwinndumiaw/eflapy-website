For about a year, most of my working days went into one client in Dubai.

They had a busy team and leads coming in from everywhere: email, Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp, SMS and phone calls. The founder was still doing client check in calls himself. My job was to take work off their plate, so I built AI systems for it.

There was an email agent that answered enquiries within minutes, including the ones that came in at night. There was a social media agent that answered comments and DMs, qualified people and booked calls. There was an SMS agent for reminders, rescheduling and following up on missed calls. There was a voice agent that ran check in calls in the founder's own voice and recorded testimonials. And there was a lead research agent that found and researched prospects, so the sales team started the day with a list instead of a spreadsheet to fill.

The systems worked. They answered people, booked calls and took the repetitive parts off the team.

Then the client's own business slowed down. They stopped getting new clients, and the work paused.

Nothing I built broke. The business around it changed, and because so much of my week depended on that one business, my week changed with it. I have spent a lot of time since then thinking about what I got wrong, and I want to write it down honestly.

## Lesson one: one client was most of the income

This is the obvious one. When one client is most of your income, you are not running a company. You are running a department of someone else's company, and you find out when their plans change.

The rule I follow now is simple. No single client should be more than about a third of revenue. If one gets close to that, the job is to go and find the next client, not to celebrate.

## Lesson two: I was selling tools, not results

When I described my work, I talked about the agents. Email agent, DM agent, voice agent. I was proud of them, and I still am. But nobody wakes up wanting an AI agent.

A managing director wants hours back. A sales lead wants every enquiry answered before a competitor answers it. An operations manager wants to stop doing the same report every Friday by hand. Those are the things worth paying for. The agent is just how you get there.

When you sell the tool, the conversation is about features and price. When you start from the result, the conversation is about the business, which is where it should have been all along.

## Lesson three: nobody knew what I did

I did good work, and I did it quietly. I almost never showed it in public. So when I needed new clients, very few people knew what Eflapy Digital did or that we had done it for companies in the UK, the Gulf, the US, Panama and Spain.

Doing the work well is not enough if nobody can see it. I knew this in theory. I study Public Relations at JKUAT. I just did not apply it to my own company.

## What is changing

Eflapy Digital is now an AI operations firm based in Nairobi, Kenya. We find where AI fits in your business, build it, and get your company using it.

We describe the work in three words: **Find. Build. Use.**

**Find.** We study how the business actually runs. How leads move, how decisions get made, where work waits, where people repeat the same task. Then we rank the gaps by what they cost and show where AI fits and where it does not.

**Build.** We build what we found, connected to the systems the company already uses, and we test it with the team on real work before it goes live.

**Use.** We get people using it. This is the part most projects skip, and it is the part I care about most now.

That last step matters because of something I keep seeing. Most companies already pay for AI tools like ChatGPT, Copilot or Claude. Very few of their people use them well. Licences sit idle. Staff are unsure what data is safe to put in. Managers do not use it, so their teams do not either. That gap is where we work.

So we are focusing on organisations of roughly 30 to 500 people that already pay for AI but are not getting much from it: SACCOs and financial services, insurance, manufacturing and logistics, law and professional services, NGOs and regional offices, healthcare and real estate.

The other change is this blog. I am going to build in public. I will write about what we are working on, what went well, and what did not. Either way, you will know what we do.
