At the start of this year, KPMG published its Global Tech Report 2026. One finding about Kenya has been quoted in boardrooms and conference panels ever since: [nearly 70 percent of organisations in Kenya aim to fully adopt AI by the end of 2026, and 26 percent have reached that point](https://www.eabusinesstimes.com/nearly-70-of-kenyan-firms-eye-ai-adoption-says-report/).

There are seven weeks left in the year. Most of the companies in that 70 percent will not get there by December. And the distance between intent and reality has [widened over the past three years, not narrowed](https://techtrendske.co.ke/?p=67151).

That gap is not a Kenyan problem alone. Across the full KPMG survey of 2,500 technology executives in 27 countries, [74 percent say their AI initiatives already deliver business value, but only 24 percent report a return on investment across multiple AI use cases, and only 11 percent consider themselves fully scaled](https://wearetechwomen.com/kpmg-global-tech-report-2026-businesses-race-to-scale-ai-despite-skills-tech-debt-challenges/).

So what is actually in the gap? Looking at the research on Kenyan and African companies in 2026, and at what we see inside organisations, it comes down to six things.

## 1. The staff are ahead of the company

Kenyan employees are not waiting for permission. PwC's research found that [64 percent of African workers are already using AI in their roles](https://www.pwc.com/ke/en/press-room/pwc-ai-performance-findings.html), against a global average of 54 percent. In East Africa, PwC put the figure at [72 percent of workers using AI in the last 12 months](https://www.pwc.com/ke/en/blog/ai-powered-future-workforce.html).

A PwC Kenya partner summed it up: [the workforce is ahead of the organisation in many cases](https://pluboard.com/2026/05/19/technology/artificial-intelligence/africa-ai-adoption-gap-pwc-research-2026/).

The problem is where that use happens. At the Safaricom Cybersecurity Summit 2026, a poll of about 150 attendees found that [42 percent knew unapproved AI tools were being used in their organisations, another 25 percent believed it was almost certainly happening, and only 6 percent said their organisation had a full AI strategy and governance policy backed by staff training](https://techtrendske.co.ke/2026/10/15/shadow-ai-business-governance/). It was a poll of attendees, not a national survey, but it matches what we find in assessments.

When staff use personal AI accounts for company work, the organisation carries the data risk and gets none of the operational benefit. Nothing they learn is shared, nothing is measured, and client information sits in accounts the company cannot see.

## 2. Use is wide, but shallow

Having used AI is not the same as working with it. Across PwC's global survey, [54 percent of workers used AI in the last year, but only 14 percent use generative AI daily](https://www.pwc.com/ke/en/blog/ai-powered-future-workforce.html). In Africa, [only 17 percent of workers report using AI agents every day](https://iafrica.com/?p=281127), and most do not use AI for workflow automation, scheduling or advanced tasks.

The same pattern shows up in paid tools. Organisations that buy enterprise licences such as Microsoft Copilot typically see [weekly active use of around 20 to 30 percent of seats](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/microsoft-copilot-enterprise-adoption-what-the-data-shows-about-real-usage-vs-hype). The rest are paid for every month and used occasionally, if at all. Most teams never set up the features that make these tools useful for real work: connectors to company systems, shared projects, custom instructions and assistants built for specific tasks.

## 3. Pilots that never leave the pilot stage

PwC's AI Performance research, released in May 2026, found that [more than 82 percent of African organisations are running AI pilots, but this is not yet translating into enterprise wide impact](https://tech.africa/?p=87711). African deployments also lean towards cost cutting, while global peers are using AI to create new revenue and redesign how value is delivered.

A pilot proves that a tool can work. It does not prove that the organisation can run it every day, with real customers, real data and real accountability. That step needs a named owner, a connection to live systems, a decision on what the AI may do on its own and what a person must approve, and a baseline to measure against. Most pilots are set up without any of these.

## 4. Data and systems that AI cannot reach

AI is only as useful as the information it can see and the systems it can act in. When the Central Bank of Kenya surveyed AI use in financial institutions, it found AI being used for credit risk, fraud, customer service and customer verification, but also warned that [critical gaps remain in data quality, governance, and management](https://allafrica.com/stories/202608250384.html).

PwC's 2026 outlook on cloud in Kenya found that data security and regulatory concerns are the top barrier for [56.7 percent of Kenyan organisations, well above the EMEA average of 31.5 percent](https://www.pwc.com/ke/en/assets/pdf/kenya-cloud-at-crossroads-from-momentum-to-maturity-2026-outlook.pdf), with complexity of integration close behind.

If customer records are scattered, if core systems do not connect, and if nobody owns the data, AI stays a chat window on the side of the work instead of part of it.

## 5. Skills, and not only technical ones

KPMG found that [53 percent of organisations still lack the expertise needed to achieve their digital transformation goals](https://www.eabusinesstimes.com/nearly-70-of-kenyan-firms-eye-ai-adoption-says-report/). PwC's 2026 Kenya CEO Survey also [identified a significant gap between AI readiness and execution, and the need for more investment in AI skills](https://capitalfm.africa/ai-revolution-or-hype-separating-fact-from-fear/).

The missing skill is usually not coding. It is knowing how to apply AI to a specific job: how a claims officer, a sales executive or a finance assistant uses it on their own tasks, with their own documents, safely. A single company wide training session does not build that.

## 6. Rules that are arriving faster than policies

Kenya already has the legal base. The Data Protection Act 2019 gives people the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that have legal or similarly significant effects. The Kenya Artificial Intelligence Strategy 2025 to 2030 was [launched in March 2025](https://capitalfm.africa/ai-revolution-or-hype-separating-fact-from-fear/), and the [Artificial Intelligence Bill 2026](https://www.ey.com/en_gl/technical/tax-alerts/kenya-publishes-artificial-intelligence-bill-2026-proposing-a-formal-framework-for-ai-governance) proposes a formal framework for AI governance, with extra obligations for high risk systems.

Kenyan organisations take privacy seriously: a Zoho survey found that [94 percent have dedicated privacy officers or teams](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/2025-11-04-kenyan-businesses-investing-more-in-ai-for-customer-service-report), the highest rate among the African markets surveyed. But a privacy officer without an AI use policy, an approved tool list and clear rules on what data may go into which tool cannot control what staff are already doing. For companies that also serve clients in the UK, the EU, the UAE or the US, the rules on AI and automated decisions in those markets apply as well.

## What closing the gap looks like

None of these six problems is solved by buying another tool. The organisations that move from plan to result tend to do the same five things, in order:

1. **Measure what is happening now.** How many people use AI, which tools, on which accounts, with what data, and how much of each paid licence is actually used.
2. **Set the rules.** An AI use policy, an approved tool list and data rules that staff understand, aligned with the Data Protection Act and the law in any other country where the company operates.
3. **Configure the tools for real work.** Connect AI to company systems, set up shared projects and assistants for each team, and decide where a person must approve.
4. **Train by role, on real tasks.** Small groups, each working on its own documents and workflows, with champions inside each team to keep it going.
5. **Measure the result.** Weekly active use, hours saved on specific tasks, use cases written into procedures, and fewer staff using unapproved tools.
